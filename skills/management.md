# Management Review

Assess the management and governance of **$ARGUMENTS**.

Over a long holding period, capital allocation determines more of the outcome than almost
anything else in the business. A company earning good returns can be ruined by a decade of
bad reinvestment, and an ordinary business can compound respectably under someone who
allocates well.

Assess management the way you would assess a business: **on the record, in numbers.**
Charisma, media profile, and a well-written shareholder letter are not evidence. Some of
the best allocators are dull communicators, and some of the worst are excellent ones.

---

## Step 1 — The capital allocation record

Every dollar of capital that management controlled had to go somewhere. Find out where it
went and what it earned. This is the core of the assessment.

Over the last five to ten years, from the cash flow statements:

| Use of capital | Cumulative | % of total | Return earned |
|----------------|-----------:|:----------:|---------------|
| Reinvestment in the business | | | Incremental ROIC |
| Acquisitions | | | Return on acquired capital; any subsequent write-downs |
| Share repurchases | | | Average price paid versus value at the time |
| Dividends | | | — (a decision, not an investment) |
| Debt reduction | | | Rate retired versus cost of alternatives |

Then judge each:

- **Reinvestment.** Did returns on incremental capital hold up as the business grew? A
  falling incremental return alongside rising capital deployment means management is
  buying growth that is worth less than it costs.
- **Acquisitions.** The most reliable place to find value destruction. What was paid, what
  was promised, what was delivered? Goodwill impairments are a confession recorded in the
  accounts. Serial acquirers deserve particular scrutiny, because acquisition accounting
  can obscure organic decline for years.
- **Buybacks.** Compare average repurchase price with your estimate of intrinsic value at
  the time. Buying heavily at the highs and stopping at the lows — the common pattern —
  destroys value and reveals that management does not value the business the way an owner
  would.
- **Dividends.** Appropriate when reinvestment opportunities are genuinely exhausted.
  A company paying dividends while raising equity or debt is doing something incoherent.

## Step 2 — Promise against delivery

Pull the stated strategy and targets from the annual reports of three, five, and seven
years ago. Compare to what happened.

| Stated (year) | Target | Actual | Acknowledged? |
|---------------|--------|--------|:-------------:|

The final column carries the most weight. Every management misses targets — external
conditions change and plans are made under uncertainty. What separates trustworthy
management is whether the miss is named plainly in the next report or quietly buried
under a new set of targets and a new metric.

**A management team that has never publicly discussed a mistake has either been perfect
or is not being candid.** The base rate for the first explanation is very low.

## Step 3 — Incentives

Read the proxy statement, not the compensation summary chart.

- **What is actually rewarded?** Adjusted EPS invites adjustment. Revenue growth invites
  value-destructive acquisition. Total shareholder return over a single year invites
  short-termism. Returns on capital, per-share value growth, and multi-year measurement
  periods are harder to game.
- **Are targets demanding?** Compare threshold, target, and maximum against the historical
  distribution of results. Targets set below trend are a transfer, not an incentive.
- **Was the plan changed after a miss?** Repricing options, resetting targets mid-cycle,
  or adding a new "strategic" discretionary component after a bad year tells you who the
  board serves.
- **Ownership.** Distinguish shares bought with personal money from shares granted. An
  executive who buys in the open market during a decline is expressing a view; one who
  sells everything the day it vests is also expressing a view.
- **Scale.** Compensation relative to peers of similar size and relative to the free cash
  flow the business produces.

## Step 4 — Governance

- **Board.** Genuinely independent, or populated with long-tenured associates of the CEO?
  Does anyone on it have relevant operating experience? Do directors own meaningful
  positions?
- **Control structure.** Dual-class shares, controlling holders, or cross-holdings. Not
  automatically disqualifying — some of the best long-term operators are founder-controlled
  — but it determines your recourse if things go wrong, which is none.
- **Related-party transactions.** Read the note. Every time. Transactions with entities
  connected to insiders are where minority shareholders are most reliably disadvantaged.
- **Auditor.** Tenure, any change and its stated reason, any material weakness disclosed,
  any restatement history.
- **Succession.** Is there an identified successor, and has the company survived a
  leadership transition before? A business that depends entirely on one person is a
  different risk than it appears.

## Step 5 — Communication quality

Read the last three annual letters and the last two earnings calls.

- Does the letter discuss what went wrong in specific, quantified terms?
- Is the language plain, or does it retreat into abstraction when results are poor?
- Is a consistent set of metrics reported year over year, or does the emphasis migrate to
  whichever number currently flatters?
- On calls: are difficult questions answered with numbers, or with strategy restatements?

## Step 6 — Verdict

| Dimension | Rating (1-5) | Evidence |
|-----------|:------------:|----------|
| Capital allocation | | |
| Candor | | |
| Incentive alignment | | |
| Governance | | |

**Disqualifying findings** — these are not weighed against strengths, because integrity
does not average:

- Evidence of dishonest disclosure or accounting manipulation
- Related-party dealings that transfer value to insiders
- A pattern of value-destructive acquisitions with no acknowledgment
- Repeated target resets that convert missed goals into paid bonuses

Conclude with a plain answer: **would you hand this team your capital for ten years
without the ability to intervene?** That is the actual question a minority shareholder is
answering, whether or not they realize it.

## Principles

- **The record outweighs the narrative.** What they did with the last billion predicts
  what they will do with the next one.
- **Acquisitions are where the evidence is.** More value is destroyed there than anywhere
  else management touches.
- **Read the proxy and the related-party note.** They are tedious and they are where the
  disclosures live.
- **Candor is measurable.** Compare what was promised to what happened, then check whether
  the gap was named.
- **Integrity is a gate, not a score.** It does not trade off against operational skill.
